Start with

\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d a^x}{dx}
\end{equation*}

We can rewrite $a = e^{\ln a}$ and therefore $a^x = e^{x \ln a}$ and this is something we can differentiate,

\begin{equation*}
    \frac{d a^x}{dx} = \frac{d e^{x \ln a} }{dx} = \ln a e^{x \ln a} = a^x \ln a \qed
\end{equation*}

Reading this from right to left, we get the integration rule,

\begin{equation*}
    \int a^x dx = \frac{1}{\ln a} a^x + C \qed
\end{equation*}


\subsection{Polynomials}

Rather simple, everything is based on

\begin{equation*}
    \int x^k dk = \frac{x^{k+1}}{k+1} + C \quad \forall k \neq -1
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Example 2.} We have

\begin{equation*}
    \int 6 \sqrt{x} - \frac{4}{x^3} dx = \int 6 x^{1/2} - 4 x^{-3} dx = 6 \frac{x^{3/2}}{\frac{3}{2}} - 4 \frac{x^{-2}}{(-2)} = 4 x^{3/2} + \frac{2}{x^2}
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Example 30.} We have

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{1}{1 - \sin x} \frac{\sin(x)}{1 + \sin x} dx = \int \frac{\sin x}{1 - \sin^2 x} dx = \int \frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x} dx = \int \frac{\tan x}{\cos x} dx = \int \tan x \sec x dx = \sec x
\end{equation*}

Here, the last integral is "knowledge".

\paragraph{Example 31.} In this example, factoring the expression is the key to the solution.

\begin{equation*}
    \int \sqrt{e^{2x} - 6 e^{x} + 9} dx = \int \sqrt{(e^x - 3)^2} dx = \int e^x - 3 dx = e^x - 3x
\end{equation*}


\paragraph{Example 33.} In a similar spirit, factoring helps

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{6x^2 + 12x + 6}{x (x+1)^2} dx = 6 \int \frac{x^2 + 2x + 1}{x (x+1)^2} dx = 6 \int \frac{(x+1)^2}{x (x+1)^2} dx = \cdots
\end{equation*}

This greatly simplifies the integrand and we obtain

\begin{equation*}
    \cdots = \int \frac{6}{x} dx = 6 \ln |x|
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Substitution}

The trick is to make a "good" substitution; just be careful, that $dx$ has to be transferred into $du$.

\paragraph{Example 41.} We start with a simple integral

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{4}{x+8} dx
\end{equation*}

We substitute $u = x+8$, from which we have $du/dx = 1 \rightarrow dx = du$ and the integral becomes

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{4}{u} du = 4 \ln|u| = 4 \ln |x+8|
\end{equation*}

The importan thing to notice is that we divide by the derivative of the substitution; this may prevent the substitution from working as in the following example.

\begin{equation*}
    \int \sin x^2 dx
\end{equation*}

Setting $u = x^2$, we have $du/dx = 2x \rightarrow dx = du / 2x$ and the integal becomes

\begin{equation*}
    \int \sin u \frac{du}{2x} = ?
\end{equation*}

This integral contains both $u$ and $x$ and therefore cannot be solved. We have hit a dead end. Checking the integral, it turns out that it cannot be expressed in closed-form.

The following integral, on the other han, is solvable by the same substitution,

\begin{equation*}
    \int x \sin(x^2) dx = \int x \sin u \frac{du}{2x} = \frac{1}{2} \int \sin u du = - \frac{1}{2} \cos u = - \frac{1}{2} \cos x^2 \qed
\end{equation*}

While we are at it, we can calculate the definitve integral

\begin{equation*}
    \int_0^x t \sin t dt = - \left. \frac{1}{2}  \cos t^2 \right|_0^x = \frac{1}{2} (1 - \cos x^2) \qed
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Example 42.} We consider

\begin{equation*}
    \int x (3x^2 + 1)^4 dx
\end{equation*}

Using $u = 3x^2 + 1$, we have $du/dx = 6x \rightarrow dx = du / 6x$ and we have

\begin{equation*}
    \int x u^4 \frac{du}{6x} = \frac{1}{6} \int u^4 du = \frac{u^5}{30} = \frac{(3x^2+1)^5}{30}
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Manipulating the Root}

\paragraph{Example 58.} When we have roots, the "usual" trick is to substitute the expression inside the root. We have

\begin{equation*}
    \int 4 x^5 \sqrt{x^3+1} dx
\end{equation*}

Setting $u = \sqrt{x^3+1}$ gives $u^2 = x^3 + 1$ which we can differentiate to obtain $2u du = 3x^2 dx \rightarrow dx = \frac{2}{3} \frac{u}{x^2} du$ which allows us to rewrite the integral as
\begin{equation*}
    \int 4x^5 u \frac{2}{3} \frac{u}{x^2} du = \frac{8}{3} \int x^3 u^2 du 
\end{equation*}

The trick is to express $x^3$ by means of $u^2 = x^3 + 1$: We have $x^3 = u^2 - 1$ and the integral becomes

\begin{equation*}
    \frac{8}{3} \int (u^2 - 1) u^2 du = \cdots = \frac{8}{15} \sqrt{x^3+1}^5 - \frac{8}{9} \sqrt{x^3+1}^3 \qed
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Example 59.} The same trick works for

\begin{equation*}
    \int x^3 \sqrt{x^2-1} dx
\end{equation*}

By setting $u = \sqrt{x^2-1}, u^2 = x^2-1$ we have $2u du = 2x dx \rightarrow dx = u/x du$. We further obtain

\begin{equation*}
    \int x^3 u \frac{u}{x} du = \int x^2 u^2 du = \int (u^2+1) u^2 du = \cdots
\end{equation*}

The other substitution option is to set $u=x^2 - 1$ from which we have $du/dx = 2x \rightarrow dx = du/2x du$ and we get

\begin{equation*}
    \int x^3 \sqrt{u} \frac{du}{2x}du = \int \frac{1}{2} x^2 u^{1/2} du = \frac{1}{2} \int (u+1)u^{1/2} du = \cdots
\end{equation*}

I don't tink that there are cases where one option substitution option actually solves the problem whereas the other option does not.

This root trick does not always work; see the following variation of Exmaple 58 from above,

\begin{equation*}
    \int x^6 \sqrt{x^3+1} dx = \frac{2}{3} \int x^6 u^2 \frac{du}{x^2} = \frac{2}{3} \int x^4 u^2 du 
\end{equation*}

Continuiing as above, we have $x^3 = u^2 - 1$ and this yields

\begin{equation*}
    \frac{2}{3} \int x \cdot x^3 u^2 du = \frac{2}{3} \int x \cdot (u^2 - 1) u^2 du
\end{equation*}

But now we have hit a dead end as we can't remove the $x$-term. The integral needs to be solved with other means \todo{which ones?}.

\paragraph{Exmaple 63.} This one is looks rathe spectacular,

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{x^5}{\sqrt{x^2 -1}} dx
\end{equation*}

However, setting $u=x^2-1$, we obtain $du/dx = 2x \rightarrow dx = du/(2x)$ and we continue as

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{x^5}{\sqrt{u}} \frac{du}{2x} = \frac{1}{2} \int x^4 u^{-1/2} du = \frac{1}{2} \int (u+1)^2 u^{-1/2} du = \cdots \qed
\end{equation*}

where we have again used $x^2 = u+1 \rightarrow x^4 = (u+1)^2$.

\paragraph{Further Example.} We can play around with integrals of the form

\begin{equation*}
    \int x^k \sqrt{1+x^2} dx
\end{equation*}

Using the substitution $u=1+x^2$, we have $du/dx = 2x \rightarrow dx = du/(2x)$ and we get

\begin{equation*}
    \int x^k u^{1/2} \frac{du}{2x} = \frac{1}{2} \int x^{k-1} u^{1/2} du  
\end{equation*}

From this we see that the integral is solvable for $k=1$; ie $\int x \sqrt{1+x^2} dx$. Expressing $x^2 = u-1$, we see that $k-1$ has to be even so that $x^{k-1}$ can be epxressed in terms of $x^2 = u-1$. This implies that the integral $\int x^k \sqrt{1+x^2} dx$ becomes solvable for $k$ being odd. \qed

\end{equation*}

The second integral requires partial fraction expansion; we have

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{1}{u^2 - a^2} du = \cdots = \frac{1}{2a} \int \frac{1}{u-a} - \frac{1}{u+a} du = \frac{1}{2a} \left( ln(u-a) - \ln(u+a) \right) = \frac{1}{2a} \ln \frac{u-a}{u+a}
\end{equation*}

The partial fraction expansion can be done manually by writing

\begin{equation*}
    \frac{1}{u^2 - a^2} = \frac{1}{(u-a)(u+a)} = \frac{A}{u-a} + \frac{B}{u+a}
\end{equation*}

and we then obtain two equation for the two unknowns $A$ and $B$. The lazier alternative is to use Maxima as follows

\begin{verbatim}
    (%i1)	f:1/((u-a)*(u+a));
    (%o1)	1/((u-a)*(u+a))
    (%i3)	partfrac(f, u);
    (%o3)	1/(2*a*(u-a))-1/(2*a*(u+a))
\end{verbatim}

Integrals E and F both require trigonometric identities; we have

\begin{equation*}
    \int \cos^2 x dx = \int \frac{1}{2} + \frac{cos 2x}{2} dx = \frac{x}{2} + \frac{1}{4} \sin(2x)
\end{equation*}

where we have expanded $\cos^2 x$; in a similar spirit, we can solve

\begin{equation*}
    \int \sin^2 x dx = \int \frac{1}{2} - \frac{cos 2x}{2} dx = \frac{x}{2} - \frac{1}{4} \sin(2x)
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Example 68.} We want to integrate

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{1}{(x+2)^2 + 25} dx = 
\end{equation*}

We substitute $u = x+2$ and have $dx = du$ and therefore

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{1}{u^2 + 5^2} du = \frac{1}{5} \arctan \left( \frac{u}{5} \right) = \frac{1}{5} \arctan \left( \frac{x+2}{5} \right) \qed
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Example 73.} We integrate

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{3x}{(x^2 + 1)^2 + 36} dx
\end{equation*}

We start with $u = x^2 + 1$ and have $du/dx = 2x \rightarrow dx = du/(2x)$ which we insert

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{3x}{u^2 + 6^2} \frac{du}{2x} = \frac{3}{2} \int \frac{1}{u^2 + 6^2} du = \frac{3}{2} \frac{1}{6} \arctan \frac{u}{6} = \frac{1}{4} \arctan \frac{x^2 + 1}{6} \qed
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Example 75.} This one is nothing new,

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{1}{x^2 - 13} dx = \int \frac{1}{(x - \sqrt{13})(x + \sqrt{13}) } dx = \frac{1}{2 \sqrt{13}} \ln \frac{x - \sqrt{13}}{x + \sqrt{13}} \qed
\end{equation*}

\paragraph{Example 76.} The integral

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{6}{(4x + 5)^2 + 10} dx
\end{equation*}

can be solved via substitution: $u= 4x+5 \rightarrow dx = du/4$ and therefore

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{6}{u^2 + \sqrt{10}^2} \frac{du}{4} = \frac{3}{2 \sqrt{10}} \arctan \frac{u}{\sqrt{10}} = \frac{3}{2 \sqrt{10}} \arctan \frac{4x+5}{\sqrt{10}} \qed
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Completing the Square}

We start with $x^2 + 8x - 1$ and want to express this as $( x + A )^2 + B$ where $A, B$ are numbers. We start from

\begin{equation*}
    (x + 4)^2 = x^2 + 8x + 16
\end{equation*}

and observe that the coefficients for $x^2$ and $x$ are correct. We only need to smuggle a constant in,

\begin{equation*}
    (x + 4)^2 + B= x^2 + 8x + 16 + B = x^2 + 8x - 1
\end{equation*}

From this we read off $B = - 17$ and we have

\begin{equation*}
    x^2 + 8x - 1 = (x+4)^2 - 17
\end{equation*}

This is useful, if $x^2 + 8x - 1$ is the denominator; we have

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{1}{x^2 + 8x - 1} = \int \frac{1}{(x+4)^2 - 17}
\end{equation*}

and this can be solved with the methods discussed previously. \qed

\paragraph{Example 80.} This is another application of completing the square; we have

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{1}{x^2 + 10x + 61} dx = \int \frac{1}{(x + 5)^2 - 25 + 61} dx = \int \frac{1}{(x + 5)^2 + 36} dx 
\end{equation*}    
    
We continue with substitution and arrive at

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{1}{u^2 + 6^2} dx = \frac{1}{6} \arctan \frac{u}{6} = \frac{1}{6} \arctan \frac{x+5}{6} \qed
\end{equation*}

\subsection{Trigonometric Substitution}

Integrals with square-roots and squares can be solved via some trigonometric substitution. Let's start with B from the book
\begin{equation*}
    \int \sqrt{a^2 - u^2} du
\end{equation*}

We make the following Ansatz

\begin{equation*}
    a \sin \theta = u \rightarrow \sin \theta = \frac{u}{a} \rightarrow \theta = \arcsin \frac{u}{a}
\end{equation*}

We also have

\begin{equation*}
    a \cos \theta = \sqrt{a^2 - a^2 \sin^2 \theta} = \sqrt{a^2 - u^2}
\end{equation*}

and finally

\begin{equation*}
    \cos \theta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{u^2}{a^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{a^2 - u^2}}{a}
\end{equation*}

Differentiating the substitution yields

\begin{equation*}
    \frac{du}{d \theta} = a \cos \theta \rightarrow du = a \cos \theta d\theta
\end{equation*}

Inserting all of this in the integral yields

\begin{equation*}
    \int a \cos \theta \cdot a \cos \theta d\theta = a^2 \int \cos^2 \theta d\theta = \frac{a^2}{2} \int 1 + \cos 2 \theta d\theta = \frac{a^2}{2} \theta + \frac{a^2}{4} \sin 2 \theta
\end{equation*}

In order to continue with reverting the substitution, we have to get rid of the $2 \theta$ in the last expression; using $\sin 2 \theta = 2 \sin \theta \cdot \cos \theta$ and the relations from the example beginning, we obtain

\begin{equation*}
    \sin 2 \theta = 2 \sin \theta \cdot \cos \theta = 2 \frac{u}{a} \frac{\sqrt{a^2 - u^2}}{a} = 2 \frac{u \sqrt{a^2 - u^2}}{a^2}
\end{equation*}

The whole integral therefore becomes

\begin{equation*}
    \int \sqrt{a^2 - u^2} du = \frac{a^2}{2} \arcsin \frac{u}{a} + \frac{a^2}{4} 2 \frac{u \sqrt{a^2 - u^2}}{a^2}  = \frac{a^2}{2} \arcsin \frac{u}{a} + \frac{1}{2} u \sqrt{a^2 - u^2} \qed
\end{equation*}

We can use this to calculate the area of a circle with center in the origin and radius $a$. We have

\begin{align*}
    \int_0^a \sqrt{a^2 - u^2} du &= \left. \frac{a^2}{2} \arcsin \frac{u}{a} + \frac{1}{2} u \sqrt{a^2 - u^2} \right|_0^a = \frac{a^2}{2} \arcsin 1 + 0 - \left( \frac{a^2}{2} \arcsin 0 + 0 \right) \\ &= \frac{a^2}{2} \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{a^2 \pi}{4} \qed
\end{align*}

Much simpler is the integral

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{du}{\sqrt{a^2 - u^2}}
\end{equation*}

With the same substitution as before ($u = a \sin \theta$), we have

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{a \cos \theta d \theta}{a \cos \theta} = \int d\theta = \theta = a \arcsin \frac{u}{a} \qed
\end{equation*}

Slightly more involved is

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{\sqrt{4 - x^2}}{x} dx
\end{equation*}

We substitute $x = 2 \sin \theta \rightarrow x^2 = 4 \sin^2 \theta$ and therefore $4 - x^2 = 4 - 4 \sin^2 \theta = 4 \cos^2 \theta$. We also have $du/d\theta = 2 \cos \theta \rightarrow dx = 2 \cos \theta d \theta$.

Inserting this into the integral, we obtain

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{2 \cos \theta}{2 \sin \theta} 2 \cos \theta d \theta = 2 \int \frac{\cos^2 \theta}{\sin \theta} d\theta = 2 \int \frac{1 - \sin^2 \theta}{\sin \theta} d\theta = 2 \int \frac{1}{\sin \theta} - \sin \theta d\theta
\end{equation*}

The second integral is trivial, the first one has "to be known"; we obtain

\begin{equation*}
    \int \frac{\sqrt{4 - x^2}}{x} dx = \ln \left| \tan (\sin \theta) \right| + \cos \left(2 \sin \theta \right) = \ln \left| \tan \frac{x}{2} \right| + \cos x \qed
\end{equation*}
